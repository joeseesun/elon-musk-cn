马斯克最黑暗的一年：无人提及的2008崩盘

Elon Musk’s Darkest Year: The 2008 Collapse Nobody Talks About

2008年12月24日，下午六点。一份融资协议在这一个小时里签完。

马斯克后来回忆，如果这笔钱晚到几天，特斯拉就会在圣诞节之后进入破产程序。

同一个十二月，他另一家公司 SpaceX 的账上也快见底了。四个月前，那家公司刚刚经历第三次发射失败。

两件事发生在同一个人身上，同一个月份。今天回头看，SpaceX 是可回收火箭和商业航天的代名词，特斯拉把整个电动车行业往前推了一大步。2008年那会儿，两家公司都可能悄无声息地消失。

还有一点容易被忽略：它们活下来，靠的既不是一份完美的商业计划，也不是某个天才发明。靠的是一连串具体到让人不安的东西，发射窗口、工程改动、投资人信心，还有几个在几乎全盘皆输时做出的决定。

他本可以在三十一岁那年退休

2008年的故事，得从更早的钱说起。

1990年代末，马斯克参与创办了 Zip2。这家公司做的生意很朴素：帮报纸把商业名录和城市指南搬到网上。那时候互联网还在长身体，很多传统企业刚弄明白网页能干什么，Zip2 站在技术和传统媒体中间，干的是搭桥的活。

1999年，康柏（Compaq）以大约3700万美元收购了这家公司。马斯克分到数千万美元。

换一个人，这里就是终点了。三十岁出头，账上几千万，买房、环游世界、做天使投资，怎么过都体面。

他直接回了科技行业。下一站是 X.com，一家做线上金融服务的公司，后来和 Confinity 合并，几经演变，成了 PayPal。2002年，eBay 用大约15亿美元的股票收购 PayPal，马斯克那一份被广泛报道为税前约1.8亿美元。

这笔钱够他这辈子不用再看任何人的脸色。

他选了另外一条路：把钱砸进两个公认最难做的行业。一个是太空，一个是电动车。

2002年，他创办 Space Exploration Technologies，也就是 SpaceX。目标写得很直白：把发射成本降下来，最终让人类成为多行星物种。听起来像科幻迷的梦，他当成商业问题在做。传统航空航天项目周期长、花钱多，如果一家公司自己设计、自己制造更多零件，成本就有下降空间。

2004年，他投资特斯拉（Tesla Motors），后来做了董事长，成为公司核心人物。特斯拉的使命同样直接：造出人们真心想开的电动车。

两个行业都不便宜。

火箭和汽车，两套不同的烧钱机器

造火箭和造网站，中间隔着一整个数量级。

软件公司出了 bug，发个更新，修好，重来。火箭不行。它需要专用硬件、大量测试、复杂物流、几个月的工程工作。一次发射失败，几秒钟内几百万美元就没了，声誉的损耗速度也一样快。客户要看火箭能不能把卫星安全送到位置，投资人要看技术是否成立，合作伙伴要看自己是不是在给一场永远做不成的工程实验买单。

特斯拉那边是另一种难。

2004年前后的电动车，电池贵，续航比不了汽油车，充电设施稀薄，消费者脑子里的印象还停留在又小又慢的代步工具。特斯拉的第一款产品 Roadster 就是要拧这个印象：一台高性能电动跑车，证明电动车可以快、可以酷、可以有科技感。

概念很漂亮，把它做成能卖的商品是另一回事。

车要能可靠运行，零件要能稳定量产，成品要过安全法规。一个看起来很小的工程问题，可能带来昂贵的重新设计或者延期。供应链、电池系统、制造流程、交付，每一环都要钱。

于是形成了一条很清晰的线：SpaceX 要钱造火箭，特斯拉要钱造车，两家公司都要在自负盈亏之前吞掉巨额投资。

而马斯克的计划，从来不是做成一件事然后收手。他要同时做成两件事。

这种雄心制造了机会，也制造了一个陷阱。往这两家公司投的钱越多，公司真倒下时，他留给自己的缓冲就越少。等到2008年，这个差别会变得非常要命。

2008年，钱本身不流动了

要理解那场危机的严重程度，得先看清它发生在一个什么样的世界里。

2008年，美国进入几十年一遇的金融危机。房地产市场崩了，银行承压，信贷市场开始冻结。投资人普遍不愿意再给那些利润不确定、还要烧好几年钱的公司掏钱。

2008年9月，雷曼兄弟（Lehman Brothers）申请破产，金融恐慌进一步加剧。

在年景好的时候，能拿到融资的公司，忽然发现自己连开口都变得困难。对成熟企业，这场危机很危险。对靠外部资金活着的年轻公司，它可以致命。

特斯拉偏偏在这个时间点，试图建起一家全新的汽车公司。

汽车是重资产行业。工厂、设备、工程团队、供应商、测试设施、销售网络，一样都不能少。软件创业公司可以靠几十个人服务几百万用户，车企不行，每一台车都要占用制造产能、质量控制资源和供应链配额。

更麻烦的是，特斯拉要挤进去的，是一个被几十年经验和大规模产能牢牢占住的行业。

而那一年，这个行业本身正在塌。

通用汽车和克莱斯勒陷入严重的财务困境，美国汽车工业的前途不明，消费者对大额支出变得谨慎。投资人有远比"给一家没量产的电动跑车公司投钱"更紧迫的事情要处理。

马斯克后来解释过，经济衰退让募资变得极其困难，而特斯拉那一轮融资正在瓦解。

同一时间，SpaceX 面对的是另一种同样致命的问题：它必须证明自己的火箭能可靠入轨。

两家公司的问题不一样，爆发的时间撞在了一起。特斯拉要钱继续开发和交付，SpaceX 要一次突破来证明承诺不是空话。而外部环境让它们都更难拿到资源。

这才是2008年的真实处境。马斯克要救的不是一家公司，是两家，而且是在全世界都不太愿意再给他一次机会的时候。

时间还在走。

猎鹰一号：三次失败，三种不同的失败

SpaceX 的早期历史，和今天人们印象里的顺风顺水没什么关系。

公司成立没几年，工程师们在做一款能把载荷送入轨道的火箭，叫猎鹰一号（Falcon 1）。和传统航空航天机构那些庞大的发射系统比，猎鹰一号体型不大，任务却不小：它要证明 SpaceX 能造出可靠的入轨运载火箭，能从商业客户和政府客户手里拿到订单。

第一次发射在2006年3月，从太平洋上的奥梅莱克岛（Omelek）起飞。发动机出问题，任务失败，火箭没有入轨。

对一家年轻公司，这个开局很难看。但火箭研发本来就难，早期失败不算异常。团队查原因，准备下一次。

第二次在2007年3月。这次飞得更远，问题出在火箭上面级，任务同样没有达到预期结果。

公司拿到了有价值的技术信息。可技术信息付不了账单。工程师还得继续做硬件，发射团队还得准备任务，公司还得有客户和投资人。

然后是第三次。2008年8月3日，猎鹰一号第三次升空。有一小段时间，任务看起来在往成功走。一级和二级分离出了问题，火箭没能入轨。

这次失败的分量完全不同。

三次连续失败之后，公司的财务状况每一天都在变得更脆弱。

把视角换到当时 SpaceX 的工程师身上：你在这件事上做了好几年，你相信技术能成，你看着同事解决了一个又一个难题，你经历过的挫折足以压垮一家意志没那么坚定的公司。现在第三枚火箭也没了。

下一次发射不能再当成一次常规试验。公司需要证明自己的活能干成，而且要在钱花完之前证明。

按当时的说法，马斯克给团队留的准备时间极短。查清失败原因、做必要改动、准备下一枚火箭、运到发射场，全都要快。第四次的赌注被抬到了最高：成了，SpaceX 终于能证明猎鹰一号可以入轨；败了，公司会在最需要新资金的时刻把剩下的信誉一起赔掉。

马斯克后来说，再失败一次就是灾难。他描述当时的状态，是公司几乎一分钱都不剩。

如果那一次再失败，公司就完了。（马斯克）

这段文字稿在第四次发射这里停住了，没有交代结果，镜头转向了特斯拉。后来的公开记录里，猎鹰一号在2008年9月28日的第四次发射成功入轨，成为第一枚由私人企业研制、进入地球轨道的液体燃料火箭。但在发射之前，SpaceX 手里确实没有第二张牌。

这里有个容易被忽略的区别。一家公司可以有一流的工程师、有野心的创始人、漂亮的技术，但如果它拿不到足够的时间和钱去证明产品能成，它可能永远等不到成功那一天。SpaceX 当时正走在这条线的边缘。

Roadster 的成本摊不开

SpaceX 在为一次入轨挣扎的时候，特斯拉在为成为一个合法车企挣扎。

Roadster 的定位很清楚：它要改变人们对电动车的看法。电动车不该是妥协，应该是人们因为真心想要才去买的东西。跑车性能加电驱动，这台车本身就是一句宣言。

从概念到商品，中间隔着一条很宽的沟。

Roadster 一路遇到工程问题、电池相关的麻烦、生产延期，还有把这家年轻公司压得喘不过气的成本。造车要求整车可靠、零件一致性可控、成品满足安全法规。这些问题在年销几十万辆的公司那里可以被摊薄，在一家只做少量高价车的公司这里，每一笔固定成本都要压在很少的销量上。

与此同时，特斯拉还得证明自己不光能交付 Roadster，还能为后续车型打好基础。交付和铺路，两件事同时要钱。

而2008年的钱，最难找。

投资人要回答的问题变得非常尖锐：在你面前的是整个美国汽车业的历史性危机，通用和克莱斯勒都在生死线上，你凭什么相信一家年轻公司能扛过经济崩塌，还要跟成熟车企正面竞争？

这个问题不好回答。特斯拉在要的，早就不是"给一项有前景的技术一点支持"，而是"赌一家创业公司能在废墟里活下来"。

马斯克把自己相当一部分个人财富投了进去，公司仍然需要新的资金才能继续运转。

这里还有一层常被忽略的结构：马斯克不是唯一的决定者。特斯拉要靠愿意追加投资的人。他们不点头，马斯克没法单方面宣布公司得救。账单要付，员工在，车要开发和交付，通往自负盈亏的路还得继续走。

账面富豪，现金穷人

马斯克故事里最容易被误解的一段，是"纸面上的财富"和"危机中能动的现金"之间的差别。

他从 PayPal 拿到了一大笔钱，然后大量投进了 SpaceX 和特斯拉。他并没有坐在一堆没动过的现金上，看着两家公司挣扎。他的财富很大一部分绑在这两家公司里。公司需要追加资金的时候，他能选的只有两条路：停止投入，保住剩下的；或者继续投入，接受个人财务状况可能变得极其危险。

他原本的打算不是这样。在后来的访谈里，他说过自己最初只准备拿一部分 PayPal 的钱投进这些事业，剩下的留着。现实走向了反面。衰退让募资变难，公司要的钱比他预想的多，最后他几乎把准备投的钱全投了进去。

2015年的一次对话里，他把这个选择说得很直白。

如果我不继续投钱，这些公司肯定会死。（马斯克，2015年）

这句话把当时的处境讲完了。继续投，是把自己也押上去；不投，是看着两家公司一起死。

每一美元只能用一次。给这边，就不能给那边。每一个决定都是取舍，而取舍的窗口正在缩小。

同一时期，他还在办离婚。个人生活里的巨大压力，叠在已经非常糟糕的财务处境上。他回忆说，2008年圣诞节前不久醒来，感觉自己正在接近精神崩溃。

2008年是我人生中最糟糕的一年。（马斯克）

圣诞夜六点，最后几个小时

特斯拉那一轮关键融资，是在2008年12月24日完成的。

马斯克说，协议大约在下午六点签完，那是这件事还能做成的最后一天、最后一个小时。

他还说，如果融资没成，特斯拉几天之后就会破产。

这个细节值得停一下。生与死的差距不是一年，不是一个月，甚至不是一周，是几天。

融资谈判拖到最后一刻，意味着公司手里的筹码已经被时间磨得差不多了。任何一方在那个下午改变主意，后面的故事就全都不存在。

而特斯拉只是问题的一半。他同时还在让 SpaceX 活着。两边的资金需求互相挤压，任何一边的失败都会把另一边拖下水。

这故事里真正能拿走的东西

把2008年拆开看，里面有几条对今天的创业者、投资人、甚至普通职场人都成立的东西。

第一条，资本是按里程碑付钱的，不是按愿景付钱的。SpaceX 缺的那一次成功发射，本质上是把"我们的技术成立"这句话变成可验证的事实。没有这个事实，故事讲得再好也没人交钱。特斯拉缺的是交付，是把车真正做出来、交到客户手里。技术公司融资的节奏，往往就卡在这类硬节点上。

第二条，同时开两条战线，风险不是相加，是相乘。单独看，SpaceX 的失败概率和特斯拉的失败概率都不低。放在一起，任何一条线断掉，另一条线也会被拖进去。资源不能复用，注意力不能复用，创始人本人也不能复用。

第三条，谈判拖到最后一天，等于把定价权交出去。马斯克在圣诞夜下午六点签字，听上去像传奇，实际上是筹码耗尽之后的结果。账上还有一年现金的时候谈，和账上只剩两周的时候谈，拿到的是两份完全不同的协议。

第四条，外部环境不进入你的模型，但它决定你的生死。2008年之前，谁都能算出两家公司需要多少钱。没人能算出雷曼兄弟会在9月倒下，信贷市场会在秋天冻结。真正的风险从来不在计划里。

第五条，也是最少被提起的一条：活下来的公司，未必是当时最好的公司。它们只是恰好在钱花完之前，把该证明的事情证明完了。时间、钱、验证，三样东西凑齐了，才有后面的故事。

如果要从这段历史里带走点能用的东西，可以看看下面几条。

  • 算清楚公司还能活多少个月，把这个数字写在你能每天看到的地方。所有决策的紧迫程度都应该从它出发，而不是从融资进度出发。
  • 为每一个融资节点绑定一个可验证的外部事实，一次发射、一次交付、一次第三方测试。让投资人看的不是 PPT 里的曲线，是已经发生的事。
  • 如果手里有两个高投入项目，先问清楚一件事：其中一个倒下时，另一个会不会被拖死。答案是会，就得提前决定优先顺序，而不是等危机替你决定。
  • 融资永远在不需要钱的时候最好谈。把下一轮的启动时间，定在账上还剩九到十二个月的位置，而不是三个月。
  • 分清账面财富和可用现金。股权、估值、期权都不是危机里能付工资的东西，只有现金是。
  • 给自己留一条与公司完全隔离的个人财务底线，这条线之下的钱，任何情况下都不动。

It depended on a series of events that came down to timing, engineering, investor confidence, and a few critical decisions made when almost everything seemed to be going wrong. To understand how Elon Musk came so close to financial ruin, we need

2:36to go back to the beginning. Because the decisions that nearly destroyed him in 2008 started years before the crisis itself. And the first clue was hidden inside a fortune that most people would have spent a very different way. Chapter 2. The fortune that changed everything. Before Tesla became a household name, before SpaceX began sending astronauts into orbit, Elon Musk had already made a fortune in Silicon Valley. But his first major success had nothing to do with cars or rockets. It started with the internet. In the late 1990s, Musk co-founded a company called Zip 2. The company developed software that helped newspapers bring business directories

3:21and city guides online. At the time, the internet was still developing, and many traditional businesses were only beginning to understand what it could become. Zip 2 positioned itself at the intersection of technology and traditional media. In 1999, Compact acquired the company for approximately $37 million.

3:42Musk, who had helped build the business, received tens of millions of dollars from the sale. He had made his first major fortune. But instead of retiring, he went straight back into the technology business. He became involved in X.com, an online financial services company that eventually merged with Confiniti. That business evolved into PayPal, the online payment platform that allowed people to send money over the internet. In 2002, eBay acquired PayPal in a deal valued at approximately 1.5 billion in stock. Musk's share of the sale was widely reported to be around $180 million before taxes and other considerations. For someone who had spent years building companies, this was

4:28a life-changing amount of money. He could have bought a mansion, traveled the world, invested in established businesses, and lived comfortably for the rest of his life. He could have chosen a quieter path. Instead, he made a decision that would define the next several decades of his career. He decided to invest heavily in two extraordinarily difficult industries.

4:52The first was space exploration. The second was electric vehicles. And neither was an obvious choice for someone who wanted to protect his wealth. Let's start with SpaceX. In 2002, Musk founded Space Exploration Technologies, better known as SpaceX. His ambition was to reduce the cost of space flight and eventually help humanity become a multilanetary species.

5:18At first, that might have sounded like the dream of a science fiction enthusiast. But Musk was approaching it as a business problem. Launching rockets was expensive. Spacecraft were complicated. Traditional aerospace projects could take years and cost enormous sums of money. Musk believed that a company that designed and manufactured more of its own technology could eventually reduce those costs. But there was one enormous obstacle.

5:46Building rockets was not like building a website. A software company could release an update, fix a bug, and try again. A rocket launch required specialized hardware, extensive testing, complex logistics, and months of engineering work. If a rocket exploded during launch, a company could lose millions of dollars in a matter of seconds, and the failure could damage its reputation just as quickly. Then came Tesla. Musk became an early investor in Tesla Motors in 2004 and later became the company's chairman and a central figure in its leadership.

6:23Tesla had an ambitious mission. Build electric cars that people would actually want to drive. At the time, electric vehicles faced serious challenges. Battery technology was expensive. Driving range was limited compared with conventional gasoline powered vehicles. Charging infrastructure was underdeveloped. And many consumers associated electric cars with small, slow vehicles rather than higherformance machines. Tesla wanted to change that perception. Its first major product was the Tesla Roadster, a high-performance electric sports car. The idea was to demonstrate that an electric vehicle could be exciting, desirable, and technologically advanced. But developing

7:08a new car was enormously expensive. The company had to engineer the vehicle, manage suppliers, develop its battery system, establish manufacturing processes, and deliver a product that customers would pay a premium to own. SpaceX needed money to build rockets. Tesla needed money to build cars. Both businesses required enormous investment before they could become financially sustainable. And Musk was determined to help them succeed. His plan was not to build one successful company and use the proceeds to retire. He was trying to build two companies that could change entire industries. That ambition created an opportunity. It also created a financial trap. because the more money

7:53he invested in those businesses, the less he had available to protect himself if they failed. And by the time 2008 arrived, that distinction would become painfully important. Chapter 3. Why the world was turning against him. To understand the severity of Musk's crisis, we need to understand the world in which it happened. In 2008, the United States entered one of the most severe financial crises in generations. The housing market had collapsed. Banks were under enormous pressure. Credit markets were freezing.

8:28Investors were becoming increasingly reluctant to finance businesses whose profits were uncertain or whose future depended on years of additional investment. Then in September 2008, Layman Brothers filed for bankruptcy. The collapse intensified the financial panic. Businesses that might have attracted investment during better economic conditions suddenly found themselves struggling to raise money.

8:54For established companies, the crisis was dangerous. For young companies that depended on outside funding, it could be fatal. And Tesla was trying to build an entirely new kind of automobile company at precisely the wrong moment. Think about the challenge. Even during a strong economy, convincing investors to fund a startup car manufacturer is difficult. The automotive industry requires factories, equipment, engineering teams, suppliers, testing facilities, and distribution networks.

9:27Unlike a small software startup, a car company cannot necessarily serve millions of customers without building physical products. Every additional vehicle introduces manufacturing costs, quality control challenges, and supply chain requirements. And Tesla was attempting to establish itself in an industry dominated by companies with decades of experience and enormous production capacity. Now, imagine asking investors to fund that business while the entire American automotive industry is facing a historic crisis. General Motors and Chrysler were experiencing severe financial difficulties. The future of the American car industry was uncertain. Consumers were becoming more

10:10cautious about major purchases. Investors had more immediate concerns than financing an unproven electric sports car company. Tesla's funding plans were coming under pressure. Meanwhile, SpaceX faced a different but equally dangerous problem. It needed to prove that its rockets could reliably reach orbit. The company had already experienced three failed attempts to launch its Falcon 1 rocket. And in the rocket business, credibility matters enormously. Customers want to know that a rocket will deliver their payload safely. Investors want evidence that the technology works. Potential partners want to know that they are not betting their money on an engineering experiment

10:52that may never succeed. SpaceX needed a successful launch to demonstrate that its approach was viable, but each failure consumed time and money, and each failure made the next attempt even more important. Musk later explained that the recession made it harder to raise capital while Tesla's financing round was falling apart. In other words, the two companies were facing different problems at the same time. Tesla needed money to continue developing and producing its vehicles.

11:24SpaceX needed a breakthrough to prove that its rockets could deliver on their promise, and the economy was making it harder for either company to get the resources it needed. This is where the story becomes particularly interesting. Musk wasn't simply trying to rescue two struggling companies. He was trying to do it when the outside world had become far less willing to give him another chance. and the clock was ticking.

11:50Chapter 4. Three rocket failures. SpaceX's early history was far from the smooth success story that people might imagine today. The company had been founded only a few years earlier. Its engineers were attempting to develop a rocket capable of delivering payloads into orbit. The vehicle was called Falcon 1. Compared with the enormous launch systems operated by established aerospace organizations, Falcon 1 was a relatively small rocket. But its mission was anything but small. It was supposed to demonstrate that SpaceX could build a reliable orbital launch vehicle and compete for commercial and government customers. The first launch took place

12:32in March 2006. The rocket lifted off from Omal Island in the Pacific Ocean, but an engine problem caused the mission to fail. The rocket did not reach orbit. For a young company, that was a painful beginning. However, rocket development is difficult and early failures are not unusual. The team investigated what went wrong and worked toward another attempt.

12:58The second Falcon 1 launch took place in March 2007. This time the rocket traveled farther, but a problem involving the vehicle's upper stage prevented the mission from reaching its intended orbital outcome. Again, SpaceX had failed to achieve its objective. The company had gained valuable technical information, but lessons alone could not pay its bills.

13:22The engineers still needed to develop hardware. The launch team still needed to prepare missions. The company still needed customers and investors. Then came the third launch. On August 3rd, 2008, Falcon 1 lifted off for its third attempt. For a moment, the mission appeared to be progressing toward success. But a problem involving the separation of the first and second stages caused the rocket to fail to achieve orbit. The failure was devastating. SpaceX had now experienced three consecutive unsuccessful orbital launch attempts and each failure made the company's financial situation more precarious. Imagine being an employee at SpaceX during that period. You have

14:08spent years working on an ambitious project. You believe the technology can work. You have watched engineers solve difficult problems. You have seen the company survive setbacks that might have destroyed a less determined organization. But now the third rocket has failed. The next launch cannot simply be treated as another routine experiment. The company needs to prove that its work can produce a successful result and it needs to do so before its money runs out. According to accounts of the period, Musk gave the team an extremely short window to prepare another launch after the third failure.

14:45The company had to move quickly. Engineers had to review the failure, make necessary changes, prepare another rocket, and get it to the launch site. There was little room for delay, and the fourth launch carried enormous consequences. If it succeeded, SpaceX could finally demonstrate that Falcon 1 was capable of reaching orbit. If it failed, the company could lose its remaining credibility at precisely the moment it needed new funding. Musk later explained that another failure would have been disastrous. He described the situation as one in which the company had virtually no money left. The fourth launch was not simply a technical milestone. It was a test of whether

15:28SpaceX could survive long enough to become a real business. And this is an important distinction. A company can have brilliant engineers, ambitious founders, and impressive technology, but if it cannot secure enough time and money to prove that its product works, the company may never get the chance to succeed. SpaceX was approaching that point. And while its engineers were preparing for another launch, Musk was dealing with a second crisis that could be just as dangerous. Tesla was running out of financial breathing room. Chapter 5. Tesla's fight for survival. While SpaceX was struggling to get a rocket into orbit, Tesla was fighting to establish itself as a legitimate

16:13automobile manufacturer. Its first car, the Tesla Roadster, was supposed to change the way people thought about electric vehicles. Instead of presenting electric cars as compromises, Tesla wanted to create a product that people would buy because they genuinely wanted it. The Roadster offered sports car performance with an electric powertrain.

16:34It was an important statement about what electric vehicles could become. But turning that concept into a commercially viable product was much more complicated than building a prototype. The Roadster faced engineering challenges, battery related difficulties, production delays, and costs that put pressure on the young company. Developing a car is an extraordinarily complex process. A company must ensure that the vehicle works reliably, that its components can be manufactured consistently, and that the final product meets safety requirements. Even a seemingly small engineering problem can lead to expensive redesigns or delays. And when a company is producing a limited number

17:17of expensive vehicles, it has fewer opportunities to spread those costs across a large volume of sales. Tesla was still trying to establish its manufacturing operation. It needed to demonstrate that it could deliver the Roadster while developing the foundation for future vehicles. That required money, a lot of money. But the global financial crisis was making that money harder to find. Investors were becoming more cautious. Potential backers had to consider whether they could afford to take risks when established businesses were themselves facing severe financial pressure. And the automobile industry was one of the clearest examples of how serious the crisis had become. Tesla was

18:00not simply asking investors to support a promising technology. It was asking them to believe that a young company could survive an economic collapse and compete against established automobile manufacturers. That was an enormous request. Musk was deeply involved in the effort to keep Tesla alive. He had already invested a substantial portion of his personal fortune in the business, but the company needed additional funding to continue operating. And there was another problem. Musk was not the only person who needed to believe in Tesla's future. The company depended on investors who were willing to commit additional capital. If they refused, Musk could not simply declare the

18:44company saved. The business still had bills to pay. It still had employees. It still had vehicles to develop and deliver, and it still needed a path toward becoming financially sustainable. Musk later described a critical financing round that came together on Christmas Eve in 2008. He said the deal closed at approximately 6:00 p.m. during the final hour of the last day it could be completed. He also recalled that Tesla could have gone bankrupt just days later if the financing had not closed.

19:18That detail captures the severity of the situation. The difference between survival and failure was not necessarily a year, a month, or even a week. It was a matter of days. But Tesla was only one part of Musk's problem. He was also trying to keep SpaceX alive. And the money he used to support one business could not be used twice. Every dollar had consequences. Every decision involved tradeoffs. And Musk was approaching a moment when he would have to decide how much of his remaining personal wealth he was willing to risk.

19:54Chapter 6. The billionaire who was running out of money. One of the most misunderstood parts of Elon Musk's story is the difference between having a large fortune on paper and having enough cash to survive a crisis. Musk had earned a substantial amount of money from the sale of PayPal, but he had invested heavily in SpaceX and Tesla. He was not sitting on a giant pile of untouched cash while his companies struggled. Much of his wealth was tied to the businesses he was trying to build. And when those businesses needed additional funding, he faced a difficult choice. He could stop investing his own money and attempt to protect what remained. But doing so

20:33could leave his companies without enough capital to survive. Or he could continue financing them and accept that his personal financial position might become extremely precarious. In later interviews, Musk explained that he had initially expected to invest only part of his PayPal proceeds into his ventures and keep the remainder. But the reality turned out differently. The recession made fundraising difficult.

20:58The businesses required more capital than he had anticipated, and he eventually committed essentially all the money he had intended to invest in his ventures. In a 2015 discussion, he described the choice in stark terms. If he did not invest more money, the companies would definitely die.
